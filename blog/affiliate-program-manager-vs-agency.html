Affiliate Program Manager vs Agency: In-House, Outsourced or Freelance?

Traffic Goat Insights - By Adam S.

At some point every affiliate program outgrows the person who launched it as a side project. Partners stop getting replies, payouts go out late, nobody has recruited anyone in months, and the founder or the marketing lead admits the program needs an owner. Then the real question arrives: do you hire an affiliate manager, sign with an agency, or bring in a freelancer for a few hours a week?

The affiliate program manager vs agency decision gets made badly in two common ways. Either a business hires a full-time manager for a program that has a few hours of real work in it, or it signs a twelve-month retainer to solve a problem that a week of fixing would have closed. Both are expensive, and both happen because the decision was made before anyone knew what the work actually was.

This post covers what each option does, indicative costs, the honest pros and cons, which option fits which stage of a program, a decision checklist, the red flags when hiring either, and where a one-off audit fits before you commit to a salary or a retainer.

A note on the numbers

Affiliate management is not priced or paid in any consistent public way. Salaries vary by country, seniority and industry, agencies quote after a call, and freelance rates span a wide range. The figures below are indicative: they reflect the kinds of offers and job listings commonly seen for small to mid-size programs, mostly in US dollars, not survey data. Use them to sanity-check a quote or a salary band, not as a rate card. The more useful part is what each option should deliver, because that is how you compare them.

What the job actually is

Whoever you hire, the work is the same. An affiliate program has six moving parts, and someone has to own each one:

  • Recruitment. Finding partners whose audience fits the product, approaching them and approving the right ones. The method is in how to recruit affiliates.
  • Activation. Getting approved partners to their first click and first sale. This is where most programs quietly lose the majority of their partners, as covered in most affiliate programs do not have a traffic problem.
  • Payouts. Setting and adjusting commission, tiers and bonuses so the program stays competitive. See affiliate commission structure for the models.
  • Retention. Keeping active partners promoting through launches, seasonal pushes and regular contact.
  • Tracking. Making sure every sale a partner drives is credited, and catching fraud and self-referral.
  • Creative and reporting. Supplying deep links, banners and product facts, and reporting the KPIs that matter to the business.

The three options differ in who does this work, how much attention each part gets, and what is left inside your business if the arrangement ends.

Option 1: an in-house affiliate manager

What they do: everything above, full-time, as an employee. A good in-house manager also does the part nobody outside can do well: working across product, marketing, finance and engineering. When the checkout is rebuilt and partner tracking breaks, or a promotion launches without partners hearing about it, or finance pays commissions on refunded orders, the in-house manager is in the room where that gets fixed. Many affiliate problems live between teams rather than inside the program, as covered in revenue leaks hide between teams, tools and funnels.

Indicative cost: a full salary plus benefits, which in the US commonly lands somewhere in the mid five figures to low six figures a year depending on seniority and market, plus recruiting time and a ramp-up period of several months before a new manager is fully productive. Tools, platform fees and commissions are on top, whoever runs the program.

Pros:

  • Full attention on your program, every working day.
  • Deep product knowledge, which partners notice and value.
  • Can fix cross-team problems an outsider can only report.
  • The knowledge, relationships and processes stay in the business.

Cons:

  • Slow to hire and slow to ramp up. A tracking defect can cost money for months while you recruit.
  • Experienced affiliate managers are hard to find, and a junior hire may need guidance nobody internal can give.
  • No outside view. A new manager inherits the program's assumptions, and an established one grades their own work.
  • A fixed cost whether the program needs forty hours a week or ten.
  • Key-person risk: if they leave, so do many of the partner relationships.

Option 2: an outsourced affiliate management agency (OPM)

What they do: outsourced affiliate program management, often called an OPM, means a specialist agency runs the program for you on a retainer. Typically that covers recruitment outreach, partner communication, payout administration, creative updates, compliance checks and monthly reporting. A good agency brings existing relationships with publishers in your niche and the pattern recognition that comes from running several programs at once.

Indicative cost: retainers are commonly quoted from around two thousand dollars a month at the low end to well over ten thousand for large or multi-network programs, often with a minimum term of three to twelve months. Some agencies add a setup fee, a percentage of affiliate revenue or a performance bonus on top. Platform and network fees are usually separate.

Pros:

  • Experienced hands quickly, without a hiring process.
  • Existing publisher relationships, which can shorten recruitment a great deal in some niches.
  • Cross-program benchmarks: they see what payouts and terms competing programs offer.
  • A team, so holidays and departures do not stop the program.

Cons:

  • Shared attention. Your program is one of many, and the hours you get depend on the tier you pay for.
  • Limited reach inside your business. They can tell you tracking is broken, but someone on your team still has to fix it.
  • Incentives that may not match yours. A retainer rewards staying engaged. A revenue share can reward volume over quality, including partners you would rather not have.
  • Minimum terms that lock in spend before you know if it is working.
  • The partner relationships may sit with the agency, not with you, unless the contract says otherwise.

Option 3: a freelance affiliate manager

What they do: a freelance or fractional affiliate manager is one experienced person who runs the program part-time, usually alongside a few other clients. In practice they often do the same work as an agency at smaller scale, with a more personal relationship and less process around it.

Indicative cost: commonly a monthly fee for a set number of hours, or an hourly rate, landing somewhere between a low agency retainer and a fraction of a full salary. Rates vary widely with experience and region, and some freelancers also take a revenue share.

Pros:

  • You pay for the hours the program actually needs.
  • You usually get a senior person directly, not an account manager in front of a junior team.
  • Flexible terms, often month to month.
  • Can be a bridge while you decide whether to hire in-house.

Cons:

  • One person is a single point of failure for holidays, illness and capacity.
  • Quality varies more than with agencies, and track records are harder to check.
  • Limited capacity for large recruitment drives or multi-network programs.
  • The same reach problem as an agency: they can recommend cross-team fixes but not make them.

Affiliate management agency vs in-house: which fits which program stage

The right answer changes as the program grows. As a rough guide:

  • Pre-launch or just launched. There is not enough work or data for a retainer or a hire yet. A founder or marketer can run it with a clear plan, or a freelancer can set it up as a fixed project: terms, tracking, onboarding emails and a first recruitment list.
  • Early, with a few dozen partners. The work is mostly recruitment and activation. A freelancer for a few hours a week, or an internal owner with a playbook, usually covers it. A full-time hire will often be underused.
  • Growing, with steady recruitment needs. This is where an agency or a fractional manager earns its fee, especially in competitive niches where existing publisher relationships matter.
  • Established, a meaningful revenue channel. The program has continuous work and the hardest problems sit between teams. This is where an in-house manager usually makes sense, sometimes with an agency or freelancer kept on for recruitment.
  • Flat or declining, cause unknown. None of the three is the first purchase. Find out what is wrong first, then decide who does the work. The diagnosis order for an underperforming program is the free way to start.

How to decide: a checklist

Work through these in order. Most programs have their answer by question four.

  • 1. Do you know what is holding the program back? If you cannot answer with evidence, that is the first job, before any hire or retainer.
  • 2. Is the fix a project or a job? Repairing tracking, restructuring payouts and rebuilding onboarding have end dates. Ongoing recruitment and partner management do not. Projects suit a fixed-scope engagement. Jobs suit a hire or a retainer.
  • 3. How many hours a week is the ongoing job? Be honest. A few hours suits a freelancer or an internal owner. Most of a week suits an agency or a hire.
  • 4. Does the work depend on other teams? If most problems involve product, checkout, finance or engineering, someone inside the business has to own the program, even if outside help does part of the work.
  • 5. Do you need relationships you do not have? If recruitment in your niche depends on knowing the right publishers, an agency or an experienced freelancer can shortcut that.
  • 6. What can you commit to for twelve months? A salary and a long retainer are both twelve-month decisions in practice. If you cannot commit, start month to month.
  • 7. Who owns the relationships and data if it ends? Whatever you choose, partner contacts, platform access and reporting should stay with your business.
  • 8. How will you measure it? Agree the KPIs before the work starts, not after the first report.

Red flags when hiring an affiliate manager

  • They talk only about recruitment. Recruiting is the visible part. Ask how they would get approved partners to a first sale and keep active ones promoting. If there is no answer, expect a large, idle partner list.
  • No questions about tracking. A manager who does not ask how sales are attributed will not notice when partners stop getting credit.
  • Vanity metrics in their track record. Partner counts and clicks are easy to grow. Ask about active partners, revenue per active partner and partner retention.
  • No view on payouts. They should be able to say how your commission compares with the programs your partners could promote instead.
  • Unwilling to work across teams. If the role needs engineering and finance cooperation, a candidate who sees the job as only partner email will struggle.

Red flags when hiring an affiliate agency

  • A long minimum term with no exit on performance. Twelve months locked in, with no agreed KPIs and no way out if they are missed, protects the agency, not you.
  • A free audit that ends in a proposal. It may be accurate, but its findings tend to point at the work the agency sells. Read it knowing who paid for it.
  • Guaranteed revenue growth. Nobody can promise a percentage increase before they have seen your data.
  • Vague hours and staffing. Ask who actually works on your program each week, at what seniority, and for how long.
  • They own the relationships. If partner contacts, platform logins or the program itself are held in the agency's accounts, leaving becomes expensive.
  • Revenue share with no quality controls. Paying the agency on affiliate volume without rules on coupon sites, brand bidding and self-referral can reward the wrong partners.
  • Reports with no actions. A monthly deck of charts that never says what changed and what happens next is reporting, not management.

A worked illustration

To make the trade-off concrete, here is an illustration with made-up numbers, not a client case. Suppose a program has 300 approved partners, of whom 25 sent a sale last quarter, and revenue has been flat for six months. The marketing lead wants to hire an affiliate manager. The CFO would rather sign a retainer.

  • If they hire first, a new manager spends the first months learning the program and working out why it is flat, on a full salary, before anything changes.
  • If they sign a retainer first, the agency spends the first month or two on its own onboarding review, which is the same diagnosis paid for at a monthly rate under a minimum term.
  • If they diagnose first and find that most of the 300 partners never got a working deep link or a reason to start, the fix is an activation project with an end date. The program may only need a freelancer for a few hours a week afterwards.
  • If the diagnosis instead finds that the program needs steady recruitment in a crowded niche, the agency or the hire is now briefed on the right problem from day one.

In every branch the work still has to be done. Diagnosing first just means the salary or retainer goes at the right problem.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to hire an affiliate manager or an agency?

It depends on how much ongoing work the program has and who will own it. An in-house affiliate manager suits a program that is already a meaningful revenue channel with continuous work and problems that sit between teams. An agency suits a program that needs experienced hands and existing partner relationships quickly but does not have enough work, or budget, for a full-time hire. A freelance manager suits smaller programs with a few hours of work a week. If you cannot yet say what the work is, find that out before choosing any of them.

How much does outsourced affiliate program management cost?

As an indicative range, not survey data, outsourced affiliate program management retainers are commonly quoted from around two thousand dollars a month for small programs to well over ten thousand dollars a month for large or multi-network programs, often with a minimum term of three to twelve months. Some agencies add a setup fee, a percentage of affiliate revenue or a performance bonus. Platform, network and commission costs are usually separate.

What does an affiliate program manager do?

An affiliate program manager recruits and approves partners, gets new partners to their first sale, keeps active partners promoting, manages commission and payout terms, supplies creative and deep links, watches tracking and fraud, and reports results. An in-house manager also works with product, marketing and finance to fix problems that sit between teams, such as checkout changes that break tracking or promotions partners hear about too late.

When should I outsource affiliate program management?

Outsourcing usually makes sense when the program has a known, ongoing workload such as steady recruitment in a competitive niche, nobody internal has the time or experience to own it, and the program is not yet big enough to justify a full-time hire. It makes less sense when the problem is a one-time fix, such as broken tracking or a below-market commission, because that is a project, not a retainer.

Should I get an affiliate program audit before hiring a manager or agency?

If the program already has partners and sales but nobody can say with evidence what is holding it back, an audit first is usually the cheaper way to decide. It tells you whether the work is a short project or an ongoing job, and how big that job is, so you can brief a hire or an agency on the right problem. If you already know what is wrong and that it needs ongoing hands, skip the audit and hire.
