How to Recruit Affiliates: A Recruitment Strategy That Finds Partners Who Actually Sell

Traffic Goat Insights - By Adam S.

Most advice on how to recruit affiliates is really advice on how to collect sign-ups: list on a network, add a "Become an affiliate" link to the footer, post in a few groups, and watch the partner count climb. The count climbs. Revenue often does not.

A program does not get paid for approved accounts. It gets paid when a partner with a real audience sends traffic that converts, month after month. Recruitment that ignores who the partner is and whether promoting you is worth their time fills the dashboard and leaves revenue where it was.

This post covers affiliate recruitment the way an operator runs it: who to recruit, where to find them, how to approach them, what your program page has to answer, and how to measure recruitment so you know it is working. It is one of the six elements in our affiliate program audit checklist, and usually the one teams want to fix first.

Before you recruit: check activation first

Recruitment is the lever teams pull when revenue stalls. Before you do, take the partners you approved three to six months ago and count how many have driven at least one sale. If most of them never started, more recruitment will mostly produce more inactive accounts. The leak is in onboarding, payouts or creative, not in the top of the funnel. Step 1: Define the partner profile

"Anyone with a website" is not a partner profile. A useful one names the partner types that can reach your buyers at the moment they are deciding, and rules out the ones that cannot.

Work through these questions for your product:

  • Where does your buyer research before buying? Comparison articles, YouTube reviews, newsletters, communities, consultants, software directories. Each is a different partner type.
  • Who already has your buyer's trust? Agencies, consultants and complementary tools often influence a purchase more than a large but general audience does.
  • Which partner types fit your economics? A low-priced product with a short sales cycle suits content and deal partners. A high-ticket product with a long sales cycle suits consultants, agencies and specialist reviewers who can carry a longer decision.
  • Which partner types do you not want? Coupon and cashback partners can claim credit for customers who were already buying. Brand-bidding paid media partners can compete with your own ads. Decide your rules before you recruit, not after.

Write the result down as two or three priority partner types, each with a sentence on why they fit. That document drives everything below, including the list, the outreach and what you put on the program page.

Step 2: Build a target list, not a sign-up form

The partners who end up driving most of a program's revenue are rarely the ones who found the sign-up form on their own. They are usually recruited one by one. That starts with a list.

Good places to build it:

  • Your competitors' partners. Search for "[competitor] review", "[competitor] alternatives" and "best [your category]". The pages that rank are written by partners who already earn from your category and know how to sell it.
  • Your own customers. Customers who write about their work, run a newsletter, consult for others or answer questions in communities are often the most credible partners you can find. They already use the product.
  • Search results for your buyers' problems. Not your brand terms, but the problems your product solves. Whoever ranks there reaches your buyer before they know your name.
  • Newsletters, podcasts and YouTube channels in your category or one step to the side of it.
  • Complementary tools and service providers who sell to the same buyer and can recommend you without competing with you.

For each name, record the partner type, the audience, a link to something specific they published, and a contact route. A list of 50 well-researched names is worth more than 500 scraped emails, because the research is what makes the outreach work.

Step 3: Write outreach that is about the partner

Most recruitment emails say the same thing: great product, generous commissions, please join. Partners with real audiences ignore most of them.

What tends to get a reply:

  • Something specific. Mention the article, video or issue you found them through, and why it made you think of them.
  • The fit, in one or two lines. Why your product suits their audience in particular. If you cannot write this line, the partner is probably not a fit.
  • The numbers a partner checks. Commission rate and model, cookie window, average order or contract value, and what kind of content converts for you. Partners compare programs on what a click is worth to them, so give them what they need to work that out.
  • A small ask. "Would this be useful for your readers?" gets more replies than a sign-up link. The goal of the first email is a conversation.
  • One or two follow-ups, then stop. A short follow-up a week later often gets the reply. A fifth email damages the relationship.

For your top partner targets, offer something more than the standard terms: an early look at a feature, a custom landing page, an exclusive offer for their audience, or a higher rate tied to performance. Step 4: Make the program page answer the partner's questions

Every partner you contact will look at your program page before replying. Many say "earn money by sharing our product" and little else.

A program page that converts the right partners answers, near the top:

  1. Who the program is for, by partner type, so the right partners recognise themselves and the wrong ones self-select out.
  2. The commission, the model (one-time, recurring, tiered) and the cookie window.
  3. When and how partners are paid, and any minimum threshold.
  4. What converts: the audience that buys, the use cases, the pages that perform.
  5. What partners get on approval: links, assets, a contact person.
  6. The rules that matter: brand bidding, coupon use, disclosure, and what gets a commission reversed.

Clear rules on the page do two jobs. They tell serious partners you run a professional program, and they cut the low-quality applications you would otherwise have to review and reject.

Step 5: Use networks and marketplaces as a source, not a strategy

Affiliate networks and marketplaces handle tracking, payouts and compliance, and put your program in front of partners who browse for offers. The mistake is treating a listing as the strategy. Listing and waiting tends to bring in many applications from partners who join many programs and promote few. Approve the ones that fit your rules, keep your effort on direct outreach to your target list, and judge the network by how many of its partners become active, not by how many apply.

Step 6: Connect recruitment to onboarding

The days after approval decide whether the work of finding a partner pays off.

  • Approve fast. A partner who applied today and hears back next week has often moved on.
  • Send a working link on day one. The welcome message should include the tracking link, the two or three assets that convert best and one clear first step.
  • Check in personally within two weeks. For recruited partners, a short message asking what they plan to publish and what they need is often the difference between a first sale and an abandoned account.

Partners who start well and then go quiet are a different problem, with different causes. We covered them in why affiliates stop promoting your program.

How to measure affiliate recruitment

Sign-ups and approved partners are the easiest numbers to report and the least useful. Measure recruitment by what it produces further down the chain:

  • Qualified applications per month: applications that match your partner profile, not all applications.
  • Outreach reply rate and join rate for your target list, by partner type.
  • Activation rate by source: the share of partners from each source (outreach, network, inbound, customers) who drive a first sale within 60 or 90 days.
  • Revenue by recruitment source after six to twelve months, net of reversals.

The activation rate by source is the one that changes decisions. It tells you which channel to put the next month of recruitment effort into. The full set of program metrics, with formulas, is in affiliate program KPIs: 12 metrics to track.

A worked example: two recruitment plans

This is an illustration with made-up round numbers, to show the reasoning. It is not a real program.

A B2B software company wants more partner revenue and has one quarter of a team member's time for recruitment. It compares two plans.

  • Plan A: list on a large network, post the program in communities and add a footer link. Over the quarter, 200 partners are approved. Most are general deal and coupon sites. Ten drive a sale within 90 days.
  • Plan B: build a list of 60 named targets across three partner types (comparison sites already reviewing competitors, consultants who implement tools in the category, and customers with newsletters), and contact each personally with a two-line fit and the numbers. Twenty join. Eight drive a sale within 90 days.

On the dashboard, Plan A looks ten times bigger. In the program, both produced a similar number of active partners, and Plan B's came from types chosen because they reach buyers who are deciding. Plan B also leaves twenty relationships to build on and forty names to revisit. The right answer is often both: keep the network as an inbound source, and put the team's time into Plan B.

Common affiliate recruitment mistakes

  • Reporting partner count as success. A growing list of inactive accounts is not growth. Report active partners and activation by source.
  • Recruiting before fixing onboarding. If new partners do not start today, new recruits will not start either.
  • Mass outreach with a template. Generic emails to scraped lists burn your reputation with the partners you most want.
  • Treating recruitment as a launch task. Partners leave, change focus and retire sites. Recruitment works best as a steady monthly routine with a living target list.

Frequently asked questions

Where do you find affiliates for a new program?

Start with people who already talk about your category: your own customers, content sites and newsletters that rank or publish on your problem, review and comparison sites, communities and tool directories, and the partners already promoting your competitors. An affiliate network marketplace can add volume, but the partners who drive most of the revenue usually come from targeted, personal outreach.

How many affiliates should a program recruit?

Fewer than most teams think. A small group of active partners usually produces most of the revenue, so the useful target is active partners, not approved accounts. Recruit in batches your onboarding can handle, measure how many of each batch drive a first sale, and only scale recruitment once that number is healthy.

Should you join an affiliate network to recruit partners?

A network can help with tracking, payouts and visibility to partners who browse its marketplace. It does not replace recruitment. Programs that list on a network and wait tend to collect many low-intent applications. Treat the marketplace as one inbound source and keep doing direct outreach to the partners you actually want.

How do you write an affiliate recruitment email that gets replies?

Make it about the partner, not your program. Reference something specific they published, explain in one or two lines why your offer fits their audience, give the numbers a partner cares about (commission, cookie window, what converts), and ask a small question rather than pushing a sign-up link. Follow up once or twice, then stop.

What should you do after an affiliate signs up?

Get them to a first click and a first sale as fast as possible. Approve quickly, send a welcome message with a working tracking link and the two or three assets that convert best, and make a personal check-in within the first two weeks. Recruitment that is not followed by onboarding mostly produces inactive accounts.
