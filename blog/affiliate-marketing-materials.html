Affiliate Marketing Materials: What to Give Affiliates So They Actually Promote

Traffic Goat Insights - By Adam S.

A new partner is approved. They log in, find a tracking link, a folder of banners from two product launches ago and a logo file. They want to write a review, send an email to their list or film a short comparison. Nothing in the folder helps them do that, so they put it on the list for later. Later rarely comes.

Affiliate marketing materials are the part of a program that turns a partner's intention into a published piece. They are also the part most programs set up once and never touch again. The result is a library that answers the question "what does our brand look like" when partners are asking "what do I say, where do I send people, and what is true about this product today".

This post covers what to give affiliates so they actually promote: the resource kit by partner type, swipe copy, the product facts sheet, deep links, comparison data, brand rules, how often to refresh, and how to measure which assets get used and which convert. Creative is one of the six elements in our affiliate program audit checklist, and in the diagnosis order for an underperforming program it comes after tracking, activation, retention and payout. That order matters: good creative cannot fix sales that are not being credited.

Why weak creative stalls activation

Most approved partners never send a click. The reasons are covered in most affiliate programs do not have a traffic problem, and creative sits close to the centre of them. A partner deciding whether to promote you is weighing effort against expected earnings. Every piece of work you leave to them, such as checking a price, finding the right landing page, writing copy from scratch or working out what they are allowed to claim, adds to the effort side.

Weak materials hurt in three specific ways:

  • They slow the first piece. The window between approval and first promotion is short. A partner who has to research your product before they can write about it often never starts.
  • They produce worse pieces. Partners working from an old pricing page or a vague brand description publish content that is inaccurate or generic, which converts poorly and sometimes has to be corrected later.
  • They send traffic to the wrong place. Without deep links, partners link to your homepage. Your homepage is built for every visitor, not for a reader who just finished a review of one feature.

The fix is not more assets. It is the right few assets for the way each partner type works, kept current.

Start with what every partner needs

Before splitting by partner type, there is a core set every affiliate resource kit should contain. If you build nothing else, build these.

1. A product facts sheet

One page, written for someone who will describe your product to their audience. It should cover:

  • What the product does, in two or three plain sentences, and who it is for.
  • Current plans and prices, including currency, billing period and any free trial terms.
  • The main features or benefits, each with a one-line explanation a partner can reuse.
  • Approved claims, with the source for each one, and claims partners must not make.
  • What changed recently, so partners with older content know what to update.
  • The date it was last updated.

The facts sheet is the single most useful asset for content and video partners, and the one most often missing. It also protects you: partners who know exactly what is true are less likely to invent it.

2. Deep links to the pages that convert

Give partners ready-made tracking links to specific pages, not just your homepage: each main product or plan, the pricing page, a free trial or demo page, any landing page built for partner traffic, and current offer pages. Label each link with what it is for, such as "for readers comparing plans" or "for email traffic". Before you publish them, check that every link keeps the partner's click ID through every redirect to checkout, using the test protocol in affiliate tracking issues. A deep link that drops tracking is worse than no deep link at all.

3. Current offers, with dates

A list of active promotions, discounts and partner-exclusive deals, each with a start date, an end date and any conditions. Partners plan content weeks ahead. If they learn about a promotion on the day it starts, most of them miss it.

4. Brand and compliance rules

Short and specific. Cover how to write your product name, which logos and colours to use, what partners may and may not say, the disclosure you expect them to include, and the activities that are not allowed, such as bidding on your brand name in paid search or using your name in a domain. Rules that are clear up front are easier to enforce than rules discovered after a piece is live, and they belong in the same written policy as your attribution terms.

5. Guidance on what converts

A short, regularly updated note on which pages, offers and angles are converting best for partner traffic right now. This is the asset partners value most and programs share least. You already have the data. Sharing it costs nothing and tells partners where to put their effort.

The asset kit by affiliate type

Partner types promote in different formats, so they need different materials. Build for the types that already drive most of your partner revenue first.

Content and SEO partners

Review sites, comparison sites, niche blogs and publishers. They write long pieces that rank and keep sending traffic for months or years.

  • The full product facts sheet, plus a feature list with plain-language explanations.
  • Comparison data: how your plans compare to each other, and fair, sourced comparisons with the main alternatives where you can stand behind them.
  • Screenshots of the product in use, at a resolution that works in an article, with short captions.
  • Answers to the questions their readers ask most, taken from your own support and sales teams.
  • A notice when anything in their published content becomes out of date, such as a price change or a renamed plan.

Email partners

Newsletter owners and partners with lists. They need copy they can adapt quickly and send on a schedule.

  • Two or three swipe emails of different lengths, each with a few subject line options.
  • A deep link for each email, pointing at a page that continues the message.
  • The offer calendar, so they can plan sends around your promotions.
  • Any restrictions on how your brand can appear in a subject line or sender name.

Social and influencer partners

Creators on video and social platforms. Their audience follows them, not your brand, so they need raw material rather than finished ads.

  • Talking points: the three or four things worth saying about the product, in plain language.
  • Short product clips and screen recordings they can cut into their own videos.
  • Image assets in the shapes each platform uses, such as square, vertical and wide.
  • A short link or a personal code that is easy to say out loud and type on a phone.
  • The disclosure requirements for sponsored or affiliate posts in their format.

Coupon and deal partners

Coupon sites, cashback sites and deal communities. They need accurate, current offer data more than anything else.

  • Active codes with exact terms, start and end dates, and the products they apply to.
  • Notice of expiry ahead of time, so dead codes do not stay live on their sites.
  • Clear rules on which codes they may publish and which are reserved for other partners.

Be deliberate about what you give this group. Coupon partners can catch customers who were already buying, and the attribution and payout side of that is covered in affiliate commission structure.

Paid traffic partners

Partners who buy ads to send traffic, if your terms allow it.

  • Approved ad copy and images, plus the claims they may use in ads.
  • Landing pages built for paid traffic, with deep links that hold tracking.
  • A written list of what is not allowed: brand-name bidding, trademark use in ad text, certain platforms or placements.
  • A named contact who can approve new ad copy quickly.

Swipe copy that partners actually use

Swipe copy is ready-made text partners can paste or adapt. Most programs either skip it or write it like their own ads, which reads wrong coming from a partner. A few principles help:

  • Write it in a partner's voice. First person, recommending rather than selling: what the product helped with and who it suits.
  • Give options, not one version. A short version, a longer version and a few subject lines or hooks let partners pick what fits their audience.
  • Leave room for their own experience. Mark clearly where a partner should add their own example or result. Copy that looks identical across ten partners helps nobody.
  • Pair each piece with a deep link. Swipe copy that ends with "click here" and no destination sends traffic to the homepage by default.

Keep the kit in one place, with dates

Partners should be able to find everything from one page, linked from the partner dashboard and from your onboarding emails. Each asset should show the date it was last updated. Expired assets should be removed, not left at the bottom of a folder, because partners will use whatever they can find.

The kit is also a natural part of onboarding. Sending the facts sheet, the top deep links and the current offer list in the first week gives new partners something concrete to publish, which is the core of the activation sequence in our affiliate activation kit.

Refresh cadence

Materials go stale in a predictable way: prices change, plans are renamed, a landing page is retired, a screenshot shows an old interface. A simple cadence keeps the kit honest:

  • On every change: update the facts sheet, offers and deep links whenever pricing, plans, promotions or key pages change, and tell partners what changed.
  • Before each season: prepare swipe copy, images and offer details for seasonal promotions early enough for partners to plan around them.
  • Every quarter: review the whole kit, retire what nobody uses, update screenshots and refresh the guidance on what converts.

Assign an owner. Creative often sits between teams, with the landing page owned by one group and the offer by another, which is a pattern described in revenue leaks hide between teams, tools and funnels. If nobody owns the kit, it will not get refreshed.

Stale materials also contribute to partners going quiet. A partner whose content now shows the wrong price either spends time fixing it or stops sending traffic to you, a pattern covered in why affiliates stop promoting your program.

How to measure which assets get used and which convert

Most programs cannot say which of their materials drive sales. That makes refresh decisions guesswork. Two kinds of measurement fix it.

Usage

  • Track views or downloads of each item in the kit, by partner type if you can.
  • Note which deep links are generated or clicked and which are never touched.
  • Ask your top partners directly which materials they use and what is missing. A short conversation with five productive partners often tells you more than a dashboard.

Performance

  • Tag each deep link and asset so clicks and conversions can be traced back to it, for example with a sub-ID or a parameter your affiliate platform records.
  • Compare conversion rate and earnings per click by asset, by landing page and by partner type.
  • Compare partners who used the kit in their first month with those who did not, on time to first click and first sale.

These sit alongside the program-level numbers in affiliate program KPIs: 12 metrics to track. Usage tells you what partners pick up. Performance tells you what is worth promoting. You need both, because a popular asset that converts poorly is pulling partner effort in the wrong direction.

A worked example: rebuilding a resource kit

This is an illustration to show the reasoning. It is not a real program and the details are invented.

A software company runs an affiliate program where most approved partners are content sites and a smaller group are newsletter owners. Its partner library holds a set of banners, a logo pack and a single link to the homepage. Partners regularly email to ask for current pricing, and several published reviews still describe a plan that was retired.

  • What it builds first: a facts sheet with current plans and approved claims, deep links to the pricing page, the free trial page and a comparison page, and a short list of what changed since the last pricing update.
  • For email partners: two swipe emails with subject line options, each paired with its own deep link.
  • For partners with outdated content: a direct note listing what is out of date in their published piece, with the corrected facts and the new links.
  • How it measures: each deep link carries a tag, so after a quarter the team can see which pages partner traffic converts on, and the next refresh starts from that data instead of opinion.

Nothing in that list is expensive. It is mostly writing down what the company already knows and putting it where partners can find it. Common affiliate creative mistakes

  • A banner pack as the whole kit. Banners suit a narrow group of partners. Content, email and video partners need facts, links and copy.
  • Everything points to the homepage. Without deep links, partner traffic lands on a page built for nobody in particular.
  • No dates on anything. Partners cannot tell what is current, so they use whatever they downloaded first.
  • Keeping conversion data internal. You know which pages and offers convert. Partners do not, unless you tell them.
  • Price changes without partner notice. Every pricing or plan change makes some published partner content wrong. Tell partners before or when it happens.
  • No measurement. Without tagged links you cannot tell which assets work, so the kit grows instead of improving.

Frequently asked questions

What marketing materials should you give affiliates?

At minimum: a product facts sheet with current prices, plans and claims partners are allowed to make, ready-made deep links to your best-converting pages, swipe copy for email and social, a small set of current images and banners, brand and compliance rules, and a short note on which offers and angles convert best right now. Then add assets for each partner type you rely on, such as comparison data for content sites or coupon terms for deal sites.

What should be in an affiliate resource kit?

A useful affiliate resource kit has one page that links to everything: the facts sheet, approved claims, deep links, swipe copy, images in the sizes partners actually use, comparison tables, current offers with start and end dates, brand rules, and a named contact. Each item should show the date it was last updated, so partners know what is current.

How often should you update affiliate creative?

Update the facts sheet and offers whenever pricing, plans or promotions change, and review the whole kit on a fixed cycle, such as every quarter. Swipe copy and images tied to seasonal promotions need refreshing before each season. Remove expired assets rather than leaving them in the library, because partners will keep using whatever they can find.

Do affiliates still use banners?

Some do, mainly on sites with fixed ad placements, but many productive partners promote in content, email, video and social, where a banner is the least useful asset you can give them. They get more from accurate product facts, deep links to the right pages, screenshots, comparison data and copy they can adapt. Keep a small current banner set, but do not let it be the whole kit.

How do you know which affiliate creative assets work?

Tag every deep link and asset so clicks and sales can be traced back to it, then compare conversion rate and earnings per click by asset and by partner type. Track downloads or views of the kit as a separate signal for what partners pick up. Ask your top partners directly which materials they use, and retire assets nobody uses.
