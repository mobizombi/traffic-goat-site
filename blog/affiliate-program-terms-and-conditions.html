Affiliate Program Terms and Conditions: What to Include and Why

Traffic Goat Insights - By Adam S.

Most affiliate program terms and conditions are written once, by copying another program's page or filling in an affiliate agreement template, and then never read again. Nobody opens them until a partner disputes a reversal, bids on the brand name or asks what happens to their pending commission after a termination. At that point the document either answers the question or it does not.

That is the wrong way to think about it. Your terms decide which sales you pay for, when you pay, what you can refuse to pay and which behaviour you can stop. Every one of those is a money decision. The terms are a revenue document that happens to be written in legal language.

This post covers what to include in affiliate terms, clause by clause, which clauses quietly cost money when they are missing or vague, illustrative plain-English wording for six of them, a short review checklist to run before launch, and when it is worth getting an outside view.

One thing first: this is operator guidance, not legal advice. It tells you what the agreement needs to decide from a commercial point of view. A lawyer who knows your jurisdiction and your industry should review the final agreement before partners accept it.

Why the terms are a revenue document

Three things make the affiliate program policy a commercial tool rather than a formality.

  • They define what you are buying. "Commission on sales" means nothing until you say which sales, net of what, credited to whom and confirmed when. Two programs with the same headline rate can have very different real costs because of those definitions.
  • They are your only basis for saying no. If a partner bids on your brand name and your terms never mention paid search, you can ask them to stop, but withholding commission is a dispute. If the terms ban it and say what happens, it is an admin task.
  • They are part of the offer. Serious partners read terms before they invest in content. Vague reversal rules, an unlimited right to change rates overnight or no word on pending commission after termination all tell a good partner to promote someone else.

So the document has two jobs that pull in different directions: protect your margin, and give honest partners enough certainty to commit. Good terms do both by being specific.

The 13 clauses that matter

A full agreement also carries standard legal sections such as liability, confidentiality, data protection and governing law. Leave those to your lawyer. The clauses below are the commercial ones that you, as the operator, have to decide.

1. Commission definition and what counts as a valid sale

State the rate, what it is calculated on and which transactions qualify. Decide whether commission is paid on the amount before or after tax, shipping, discounts and store credit. Decide whether it applies to new customers only or to repeat purchases, to all products or a list, and to the first payment or recurring payments for a set period. Say plainly that orders placed by the partner themselves do not qualify, if that is your rule. The choice of model and rate is a separate subject, covered in affiliate commission structure. The terms only need to state the outcome without ambiguity.

2. Cookie window and attribution rule

Give the length of the tracking window and the rule for deciding who gets the credit when more than one partner or channel touched the customer. Last click is the most common default, but say so, and say how affiliate clicks rank against your own paid search, email and retargeting. If a coupon code can override a click, or a click can override a code, write down which wins. Also state what happens when tracking fails: whether you accept manual claims, what evidence a partner needs and the deadline for raising one. The practical side of this is in affiliate tracking issues.

3. Validation period, reversals and clawbacks

A commission should move through clear states: pending, approved, paid. The terms set how long it stays pending and what can reverse it. List the reversal reasons: refunds, cancellations, chargebacks, duplicate orders, failed payments, fraud and breaches of the terms. Then deal with the harder case, commission that has already been paid on an order that later fails. The usual tool is a right to deduct it from future earnings. How validation windows help you catch bad traffic before payout is covered in affiliate fraud detection. In the terms, the job is to make sure the right exists and that partners can see the reason for each reversal.

4. Payment threshold, schedule and currency

State the minimum payout, the payment date, the methods you support and the currency. Say who carries transfer fees and conversion costs. Say what the partner must supply before you can pay, such as tax forms or an invoice, and what happens to a balance that sits below the threshold. A predictable date matters more to partners than a fast one, so only promise a schedule your finance team can actually keep.

5. Brand bidding and paid search policy

This is the clause most often missing or too thin. A one-line "no brand bidding" rule leaves gaps. Decide and state each of these: whether partners may bid on your brand name, product names and misspellings; whether they may bid on brand plus another word such as "review" or "discount"; whether your brand may appear in ad text or the display URL; whether ads may link straight to your site or must go to the partner's own page; and whether partners must add your brand as a negative keyword. If you allow selected partners to run brand campaigns, say that it requires written approval.

6. Coupon and deal-site rules

Say which codes a partner may promote: only codes issued to them, or any public code. Ban pages that advertise codes that do not exist and "click to reveal" mechanics that set a cookie without showing a real offer. Decide whether an order that uses a code not assigned to the partner earns commission, and whether coupon and cashback partners are on a different rate. If browser extensions and toolbars need separate approval, say so here.

7. Prohibited traffic sources

List what is not allowed rather than relying on a general "ethical marketing" line. Typical entries are unsolicited email and messaging, incentivised traffic where visitors are rewarded for buying or signing up, pop-unders and forced redirects, cookie dropping without a real click, automated traffic, misleading claims, and placement next to content your brand cannot sit beside. If your industry limits which countries or audiences you can market to, state those limits here as well. A banned-list is more useful than a long allowed-list, because new channels appear faster than you can update the page.

8. Disclosure and advertising compliance

Regulators in many markets, including the FTC in the United States, expect a clear disclosure when someone is paid to recommend a product. Your terms should require partners to disclose the relationship clearly and close to the recommendation, to follow the advertising and privacy rules of the places they market in, and to make only claims you have approved. This protects customers, and it also protects you, because a brand can be held responsible for what its partners say. Which rules apply to you is a question for your lawyer.

9. Creative and brand asset usage

Grant a limited licence to use your name, logo and approved materials for promoting the program, and say what partners may not do: alter logos, imply they are you, register domains or social handles containing your brand, or keep using out-of-date offers after you have withdrawn them. Give a period for removing expired promotions. What to put in the asset kit itself is in affiliate marketing materials.

10. Sub-affiliates and networks

If a partner sends traffic through other publishers, you are one step removed from the people actually promoting you. Decide whether sub-affiliates are allowed at all, whether they need approval, and whether the partner must reveal the source of each sale on request. Make the main partner responsible for everything their sub-affiliates do, including reversals.

11. Termination and pending commissions

Either side should be able to end the agreement with notice. The part that causes disputes is the money in the pipeline. Separate two cases. If the agreement ends without a breach, the fair and usual position is that commission on valid sales made before the end date is still validated and paid on the normal schedule. If it ends because of fraud or a serious breach, you will want the right to withhold unpaid commission connected to that breach. Also say what happens to recurring commissions after termination, and require links and brand assets to come down.

12. Right to change terms and the notice period

You need the right to change rates and rules. Partners need to know a change will not be applied to work they have already done. State how you will notify partners, how much notice you give, and that changes apply to sales made after the effective date. An emergency exception for legal or fraud reasons is reasonable. An unlimited right to change anything without notice is legal in many places and still a bad idea commercially, because the partners you most want will not build on it.

13. Inactive accounts

Define inactivity, for example no clicks or no sales for a set period, and say what follows: a warning email, then closure, and what happens to a small unpaid balance. If you pay recurring or lifetime commission, decide whether a partner who has stopped promoting keeps earning indefinitely. This clause is easy to skip, and the cost only shows up years later.

The clauses that quietly cost money when missing or vague

Not all thirteen carry the same risk. These are the gaps that turn into recurring cost without anyone noticing.

  • No definition of the commission base. If the terms say "20% of sales" and the platform calculates on the order total, you may be paying commission on tax, shipping and the discounted portion of the order. On thin margins that difference is material.
  • A validation period shorter than the refund window. If customers can return a product after the commission has been approved and paid, every late refund is a commission you paid for nothing, and recovering it is a chore.
  • No stated attribution rule. Without one, the platform default decides, which is usually last click with no exceptions. The partner who appears at checkout takes the credit from the partner who created the demand, and from your own channels.
  • A brand bidding rule with holes. Banning the exact brand name but not misspellings, brand-plus-keyword or use of the brand in ad text leaves most of the practice open.
  • No rule on unassigned coupon codes. Leaked codes spread to every coupon site, and you pay commission plus discount on customers who were already at the checkout.
  • No right to deduct from future earnings. Without it, a chargeback that lands after payout is simply a loss.
  • Silence on existing customers and self-purchase. You pay acquisition commission on people you already had.
  • Lifetime commission with no activity condition. A liability that grows every month and is very hard to remove later without a fight.
  • No notice mechanism for changes. When you need to fix any of the above, you have no clean way to do it, so the old rule stays.

Gaps on the other side cost money too, just less visibly. Unexplained reversals, an unreliable payment date and one-sided change clauses are among the main reasons good partners go quiet, which is covered in why affiliates stop promoting your program.

Example wording for six key clauses

The examples below are illustrative only. They show the level of detail that removes ambiguity, in plain English. They are not legal advice and not a finished affiliate agreement template. Every number in them (percentages, days, amounts) is a made-up placeholder, not a recommendation or a benchmark. Have a lawyer adapt the wording to your business and jurisdiction.

Example: valid sale and commission base

"Commission is 20% of the net sale value of each Qualifying Sale. Net sale value means the amount the customer paid for products, excluding taxes, shipping, gift cards and any amount later refunded. A Qualifying Sale is a completed purchase by a new customer who reached our site through your tracking link within the tracking period. Purchases made by you, your employees or members of your household are not Qualifying Sales."

Example: attribution

"The tracking period is 30 days from the customer's most recent click on an affiliate link. Where more than one affiliate link was clicked, the most recent click receives the commission. Where an order uses a coupon code assigned exclusively to one affiliate, that affiliate receives the commission regardless of clicks. If you believe a sale was not tracked, tell us within 30 days of the order date with the order details, and we will review it."

Example: validation and reversals

"Commissions are pending for 45 days from the order date, then approved. We may reverse a pending commission if the order is refunded, cancelled, charged back, duplicated or unpaid, or if it resulted from a breach of these terms. Each reversal will show a reason in your dashboard. If an order is refunded or charged back after the commission has been paid, we may deduct that commission from your future payments."

Example: brand bidding

"Without our written approval you may not bid on our brand name, our product names, or misspellings or variations of them, alone or combined with other words, in any paid search or paid social campaign. You may not use our brand name in ad headlines, ad text or display URLs. Paid ads may not link directly to our site. You must add our brand name as a negative keyword in your campaigns. Commission on sales generated in breach of this clause will be reversed."

Example: termination and pending commission

"Either party may end this agreement at any time with 14 days' written notice. Commissions on Qualifying Sales made before the end date will be validated and paid on the normal schedule. If we end this agreement because of fraud or a material breach of these terms, we may withhold unpaid commissions connected to that breach. After the end date you must remove your tracking links and our brand assets within 14 days."

Example: changes to the terms

"We may change these terms, including commission rates. We will email the address on your account at least 30 days before a change takes effect. Changes apply only to sales made on or after the effective date. Where a change is required by law or needed to stop fraud, we may apply it immediately and will tell you as soon as we can. Continuing to promote us after the effective date means you accept the change."

Notice what these have in common: a number where a number is needed, a defined term where a word could be read two ways, and a stated consequence. That is most of what separates terms you can enforce from terms you can only point at.

A pre-launch review checklist

Run this before you publish new terms or relaunch a program. It takes an hour or two and needs the person who runs the program, someone from finance and whoever manages the platform.

  • Platform match. Do the cookie window, commission base, validation period and attribution rule configured in the platform match the written terms exactly? A mismatch here is the most common finding, and the platform always wins in practice.
  • Refund window check. Is the validation period at least as long as your refund or cancellation window, plus time for your own review?
  • Margin check. Work one order through by hand: commission, discount, any network fee and payment costs, on your lowest-margin product. Is it still an order you want?
  • Stacking check. Can one order trigger an affiliate commission, a referral reward, cashback and a discount at the same time? Do the terms say which one applies?
  • Finance sign-off. Can the payment date, threshold, currencies and methods in the terms actually be delivered every month?
  • Plain-language summary. Is there a short summary of the key rules on the signup page? Partners should not need the full agreement to learn the rate, the window, the payment date and the main exclusions.
  • Acceptance record. Does signup record that each partner accepted the terms, with a date and version? Without that, enforcing any clause is harder.
  • Change process. Do you have a working way to email every partner, and a version history of the terms?
  • Enforcement owner. Is one named person responsible for checking brand bidding, coupon use and reversals each month? A rule nobody checks is not a rule.
  • Legal review. Has a lawyer reviewed the final text, including the sections this post does not cover?

If you are reviewing a live program rather than launching one, add a step: pull the last three months of reversals and disputes and check whether the terms answered each one. The ones they could not answer are your edit list.

When an audit is worth it

Writing terms is a job for you and your lawyer. Knowing whether the terms match how the program actually behaves is a different job, and that is where an outside audit earns its fee. It makes sense when:

  • The terms are older than the program's current shape. New partner types, new products or a platform migration since the terms were written.
  • Commission cost is rising faster than revenue and the terms do not explain why.
  • Disputes keep recurring on the same subject, usually reversals, attribution or coupon credit.
  • You are about to change rates or rules and want to know what the change will do to partners before you announce it.
  • Nobody is sure what the platform is configured to do.

Frequently asked questions

What should affiliate program terms and conditions include?

At a minimum: the commission rate and what counts as a valid sale, the cookie window and attribution rule, the validation period and the reasons a commission can be reversed, payment threshold, schedule and currency, the paid search and brand bidding policy, coupon rules, prohibited traffic sources, disclosure and compliance duties, how brand assets may be used, sub-affiliate rules, termination and what happens to pending commission, how terms can be changed and with how much notice, and what happens to inactive accounts.

Can I use an affiliate agreement template?

A template is a reasonable starting point for structure and for the standard legal sections. It will not make the commercial decisions for you: your commission base, validation period, attribution rule, brand bidding policy and termination terms have to reflect your own margins, refund window and platform settings. Fill those in deliberately, check that the platform is configured to match, and have a lawyer review the final version.

Do affiliate terms and conditions need to be reviewed by a lawyer?

Yes. The agreement is a contract, and it touches advertising rules, data protection, tax and liability, all of which vary by country and industry. An operator can and should decide the commercial rules, but a lawyer who knows your jurisdiction should turn them into the final text. Guidance like this post is not a substitute for that review.

Can I change affiliate program terms after partners have joined?

Usually yes, if the terms give you that right and you follow the process they describe. Good practice is to notify every partner in writing, give a fixed notice period, and apply the change only to sales made after the effective date. Changing rates or rules on sales that have already happened damages trust quickly and may not be enforceable, so check with your lawyer before you do it.

What happens to pending commissions when an affiliate is terminated?

It depends on what the terms say, which is why the clause matters. A common approach is that commission on valid sales made before the end date is still validated and paid on the normal schedule when the agreement ends without a breach, while commission connected to fraud or a serious breach can be withheld. If the terms are silent, you are left negotiating each case, so set the rule before you need it.
