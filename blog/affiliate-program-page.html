Affiliate Program Page: What to Put on It and the Contradictions That Cost Sign-ups

Traffic Goat Insights - By Adam S.

An affiliate program page has one job: let a good partner decide to apply in under a minute. It must state the rate, model, recurring length, cookie window, when commission starts, and how and when payouts happen, then show one clear way to apply. Every number must match your terms, FAQ and network listing exactly.

Most SaaS teams put real effort into the product pages and very little into the page that recruits the people who sell the product. The affiliate program landing page gets written once at launch, the terms get updated a year later, an FAQ gets added by someone else, the program moves to a new affiliate network, and nobody reads all four side by side again. The result is rarely a missing page. It is a page that says one thing while the terms say another. This post covers what the page must say, why every source has to agree, and how to find the contradictions before a prospective partner does.

Why the affiliate program page decides who applies

Experienced partners treat your program page as a first test of how the program is run. They are not reading it for inspiration. They are checking a short list of facts against the other programs in their niche, and they are looking for reasons not to bother. A missing number is a reason. Two different numbers are a stronger one, because they suggest that whatever is promised now may not be what gets paid later.

The partners you most want, the ones with an audience and a choice of programs, are also the ones most likely to read carefully and least likely to email you to ask. They simply move on. That is why a flawed page rarely shows up as complaints. It shows up as fewer applications from good partners, and as applications from people who did not read anything at all. Getting the page right is part of recruitment, which we cover end to end in how to recruit affiliates.

What an affiliate program page must answer

Put these near the top, as plain numbers and short sentences. A partner should be able to find each one without opening the terms.

  • The commission rate. One number, or a clear table if you have tiers. "Up to" figures are fine only if the page also shows what the starting rate is and what it takes to reach the top.
  • The model. One-time, recurring, a flat bounty per signup, or a hybrid. Say which revenue it applies to: the first payment, every payment, upgrades, annual plans. The trade-offs between models are in affiliate commission structure.
  • How long recurring commission lasts. A fixed number of months, a fixed number of billing cycles, or the life of the customer. If it is "lifetime", say whether that means the customer's lifetime, the partner's time in the program, or both.
  • The cookie window. How long after a click a signup still counts, and what happens if the customer clicks a second partner's link.
  • When commission starts. The exact event: a signup, a trial start, or the first paid invoice. If you offer a free trial, this is the line partners read most closely.
  • Payout timing, threshold and method. How long a commission stays pending, the day of the month you pay, the minimum balance, and the payment methods you support.
  • Who the program is for. The partner types that do well with you, and any you do not accept. This saves you reviewing applications you will reject.
  • How to apply and what happens next. One button, one destination, and a sentence on how long approval takes and what the partner receives when approved.
  • A contact. An address or form that reaches a person who replies. Partners with questions about a deal size or a custom page need somewhere to ask.

Customer discounts for referred users, approved promotion methods and the main restrictions (brand bidding, coupon sites, disclosure) belong on the page too, in a short section, with the detail left to the terms.

One source of truth: page, terms, FAQ and network listing must match

A typical SaaS program publishes the same facts in four or more places:

  • the program page on your own site;
  • the affiliate terms and conditions;
  • an FAQ, either on the program page or in your help center;
  • the listing or signup page on your affiliate network or platform;
  • and often a PDF guide, a welcome email and the partner dashboard.

Each was usually written at a different time by a different person. The rule that prevents contradictions is simple to state: pick one document as the source of truth for every commercial number, and make every other place copy from it. In practice the terms are the natural source, because they are what you are bound by. What goes in them, and how to word the clauses, is covered in affiliate program terms and conditions: what to include and why.

Then keep a short list of every place each number appears, so a change to the terms triggers a change everywhere else. Without that list, the update happens in the one place the person making it knows about, and the rest drift.

The contradiction checklist

These are the patterns that come up again and again when you read a program page, its terms, its FAQ and its signup page side by side. None of them is unusual. Each one gives a careful partner a reason to leave.

Common affiliate program page contradictions and how to fix them
ContradictionWhy affiliates bounceFix
Different commission rate in two placesThe partner cannot tell which rate will be paid, and assumes the lower one.Set the rate in the terms and copy the same number, word for word, to every other source.
Headline rate higher or lower than the tier tableA hero line that says "up to" one figure while the top tier shows another reads as careless or misleading.Derive the headline from the table: the starting rate and the real top rate.
Recurring length differs (12 months vs lifetime vs first payment only)This changes the value of a referral several times over, so partners treat it as the deciding fact.State the length once as a number of months or billing cycles, and repeat it exactly.
Commission start differs (signup vs first paid invoice)With a free trial in between, the two readings pay a different number of months.Name one event, define it in the terms, and use the same phrase on the page and in the FAQ.
Payout timing differs within one answer or between page and termsPartners plan around when money arrives; two dates suggest nobody knows.Give the hold period and pay date as separate, simple facts, per plan type if they differ.
Overlapping or misnumbered tiersIf a sales count fits two tiers, or two tiers share a name, the partner cannot work out what they would earn.Make tier ranges continuous with no overlap, number them in order, and test the boundaries.
A worked example that does not add upPartners check the arithmetic; a wrong total undermines every other number on the page.Recalculate every example after each change, and show the multiplication.

Most of these are not policy problems. The policy is usually fine. The page simply stopped matching it after one of the sources changed.

A 15-minute self-check

Do this as if you were a partner who has never heard of you. Use a private browser window and do not log in.

  1. Minutes 1 to 3: find the page. Start from your homepage and look for the affiliate or partner link in the header and footer. Then type /affiliate, /affiliates, /affiliate-program and /partners after your domain. Note where each one lands.
  2. Minutes 3 to 6: write down the facts. From the program page only, write the rate, model, recurring length, cookie window, commission start event, hold period, pay date, threshold and payment methods. Leave a blank for anything you cannot find.
  3. Minutes 6 to 9: compare the terms. Open the affiliate terms and fill in a second column for the same facts. Mark every row where the two columns differ in wording, not just in number.
  4. Minutes 9 to 11: compare the FAQ and the network listing. Add a third and fourth column. Check the signup form copy too; it often has its own one-line summary of the offer.
  5. Minutes 11 to 13: check the arithmetic. Recalculate any worked example and check that tier ranges do not overlap.
  6. Minutes 13 to 15: follow the apply path. Click every apply button on the page. Each should reach the same, current signup. Note anything that lands on an old network, a login screen or an error.

Any blank in the first column is a missing answer. Any row where the columns differ is a contradiction. Fix both by updating the source of truth first and copying from it.

Dead URL hygiene: /affiliate, /affiliates and /partners

Partners and affiliate directories guess URLs. They type your domain plus /affiliate, they follow a link from an old roundup post, or they click a footer link that was set up three site redesigns ago. Every one of those paths should end on the current program page with a working apply button.

  • Cover the common paths. /affiliate, /affiliates, /affiliate-program, /affiliate-programme, /referral and /partners (unless /partners is a separate agency or integration program, in which case it should link clearly to the affiliate program).
  • Redirect in one step. A permanent redirect straight to the current page, not a chain through an old URL and the homepage.
  • Never redirect to the terms alone. A terms page with no rate summary and no apply link is a dead end, even though it returns a working status code.
  • Do not send it to the homepage. A partner who asked for the affiliate program and got the homepage usually concludes the program has closed.
  • Close a program on purpose. If you have ended the program, say so on the old URL with a short notice, rather than a 404 that looks like an accident.
  • Check the platform side. When you move affiliate platforms, the old signup page and the old network listing can stay live. Turn them off or point them at the new signup.

The same discipline applies to the links your partners already use, which is covered in how to audit affiliate links.

An illustration: one page, three numbers

This is a made-up example to show the reasoning. It is not a real company or program.

A subscription tool offers a 14-day free trial. Its program page says "20% recurring for 12 months". The FAQ, written later, says the 12 months run "from your referral's first paid invoice". The terms, updated by legal, say commission is paid "for 12 months after sign-up". A partner reading carefully sees that if the 12 months are counted from sign-up, the 14-day trial eats into them, so the two readings can differ by a billing cycle. They also see that the network listing still shows the old 25% launch rate.

Nothing here is a bad policy. But a partner comparing this program with two others now has three questions and no one to ask, and the other programs answered theirs on the first screen. The fix takes an afternoon: choose the start event, write it into the terms, copy the same sentence to the page and FAQ, update the listing, and add the four sources to a shared change list.

Common affiliate program page mistakes

  • No number on the page. "Earn recurring commission" without a rate asks partners to apply before they know what they are applying for.
  • Copy that outlived the offer. Launch rates, expired promotions and old copyright years tell partners nobody is maintaining the page.
  • A rule that contradicts the pitch. Inviting bloggers and social creators while the terms ban sharing links on blogs and social media.
  • Vague payout terms. "Subject to a minimum threshold" with no amount, or "paid monthly" with no day.
  • An apply path you have not clicked lately. Buttons that lead to a retired network, a broken form or a page that requires an existing customer login.
  • Updating one source only. Changing the terms without changing the page, FAQ, PDF guide and listing is how most contradictions are born.

Frequently asked questions

What should an affiliate program page include?

The commission rate and model, how long recurring commission lasts, the cookie window, the exact event that starts commission, payout timing, threshold and method, who the program is for, how to apply and what happens next, and a named way to contact the team. Each of these should appear as a plain number or sentence, not only inside the terms.

Should the commission rate be on the program page or only in the terms?

On the page. Partners compare programs before they read any terms, and a page without a number loses to one that has it. The terms should repeat the same number and add the conditions. If the rate is only visible on an affiliate network listing or inside the dashboard after approval, many partners will not get that far.

What is the most common affiliate program page mistake?

A contradiction between the page and another source: the terms, the FAQ, the signup form or the network listing. Typical cases are a different rate or recurring length, a different start point for the commission period, and payout timing that changes from one answer to the next. Each one makes a careful partner stop and wonder which version will be honoured.

When does affiliate commission start if the product has a free trial?

That is your decision, but you must state it the same way everywhere. The usual choices are the signup date or the first paid invoice. With a trial in between, a 12-month window counted from signup can pay fewer months than one counted from the first payment, so partners notice the difference and need one clear answer.

Which URLs should point to the affiliate program page?

At minimum /affiliate, /affiliates, /affiliate-program and /partners if you do not use it for something else. Each should load the current program page or redirect to it in one step. None should return a 404, land on the homepage, or open an old terms page with no way to apply.

How often should you check your affiliate program page?

Every time the commission, terms, payout process or affiliate platform changes, and on a fixed schedule, such as once a quarter, even when nothing has changed. The quarterly check is the one that catches the FAQ nobody updated and the old URL that started returning an error.
