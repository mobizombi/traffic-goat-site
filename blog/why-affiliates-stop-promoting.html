Why Affiliates Stop Promoting Your Program (and How to Find Out Which Reason Is Yours)

Traffic Goat Insights - By Adam S.

Affiliates stop promoting a program for a small number of reasons: they earn more per click somewhere else, they stop trusting your tracking or payouts, your offer or landing page converts worse than it used to, or nobody on your side noticed they were slipping. They almost never tell you. They just move your link lower on the page, swap it out of their email rotation, or replace it with a competitor's, and your dashboard shows a slow decline that looks like "the market".

This post is about partners who were productive and went quiet. That is a different problem from partners who sign up and never send a click, which we covered in most affiliate programs do not have a traffic problem. Losing an active partner usually hurts more, because their traffic was already proven.

Why it happens quietly

A serious affiliate is running a small media business. Every placement on their site, every email slot and every video description is inventory, and they allocate it to whatever earns the most for the least hassle. When your program stops winning that comparison, they do not send a resignation letter. They reallocate.

That is why "affiliate program not growing" is so often misdiagnosed. The total number of partners may even be going up, while the handful who drive most of the revenue are slowly giving your competitors the better spots. If you only watch program-level totals, you see a plateau, not a departure.

The seven reasons affiliates stop promoting

1. They earn more per click elsewhere

Affiliates compare programs on earnings per click, not on the headline commission rate. A higher percentage means nothing if your conversion rate dropped, your average order value is lower, or your cookie window is shorter than the alternative. If a competitor launched a better offer, raised rates, or simply converts better, your best partners noticed before you did.

2. They stopped trusting your tracking

Partners watch their own numbers closely. When their clicks hold steady but reported conversions fall, or when a coupon code or app install does not get credited, they assume the worst. Even if the cause is an honest technical gap, the effect is the same: they send their traffic where they feel credited. This is one of the most common silent causes, and one of the least measured.

3. Payouts became slow, unpredictable or contested

Late payments, raised thresholds, long hold periods, or reversals without a clear explanation all tell a partner that their earnings are at risk. Professional affiliates have cash flow to manage. A program that pays reliably and explains every reversal earns a lot of patience. One that does not loses its best partners first, because they have the most options.

4. Your offer or landing page got worse

Sometimes the affiliate program did not change at all, but the product did. A pricing change, a new checkout step, a redesigned landing page, a removed free trial or a weaker promotion can lower conversion for every partner at once. The marketing team ships the change, the affiliate team never hears about it, and partners see their earnings drop. We wrote about how often this kind of leak hides between teams, tools and funnels.

5. Nobody talked to them

Top partners who only receive the same newsletter as the long tail eventually conclude they are not valued. Meanwhile, a competing program's manager is calling them, offering early access to promotions and a custom landing page. Relationship is not a soft factor in affiliate marketing. For your top partners, it is often the deciding one.

6. Their creative and information went stale

A content site cannot keep recommending a product with outdated screenshots, an old price or a promotion that ended months ago. Email and social partners need fresh angles. If partners have to dig for current information or build every asset themselves, the easier program wins the placement.

7. Terms changed against them

New restrictions on brand bidding, coupon use, traffic sources or commission on returning customers can make a partner's existing model unprofitable overnight. Sometimes these changes are right for the business. But if they were made without talking to the partners affected, expect those partners to leave without a word.

A diagnostic you can run this week

You do not need new software for a first pass. You need an export from your affiliate platform and an hour of honest work.

  1. List your top 20 partners by revenue over the last 12 months. For each one, compare their last 60 days with their own previous 60 days. Flag anyone whose revenue or clicks fell meaningfully.
  2. Split each flagged partner's decline into clicks and conversion rate. If clicks fell, they moved your placement (reasons 1, 2, 5, 6 or 7). If clicks held but conversion fell, the problem is on your side of the click (reasons 2 or 4).
  3. Check whether their site or channel is still active. If they are still publishing and still promoting other programs in your category, you lost the allocation, not the partner.
  4. Reconcile conversions with your own sales data. Pick a recent period and compare what your affiliate platform credited with what your billing or order system recorded for referred customers. A gap you cannot explain is a tracking issue until proven otherwise.
  5. Review payout history for the flagged partners. Look for late payments, reversals and support tickets about missing commissions in the months before their decline.
  6. List every change to your offer, pricing, checkout or terms in the last six months. Line the dates up against the declines. Coincidences are worth a closer look.
  7. Ask them. Not a generic check-in, but a specific question: "Your clicks to us dropped since spring. Did our conversion rate change on your side, or did another program become a better fit?" Specific questions get honest answers far more often than "just touching base".

What to fix first

The diagnostic will usually point to one or two causes. Fix in this order:

  • Tracking and payouts first. Nothing else works if partners do not trust that they will be credited and paid. These are also usually the cheapest to fix.
  • Conversion on your side next. If your landing page or offer got worse, a higher commission is just paying more for a weaker product. Fix the page, then tell partners exactly what changed.
  • Relationship and creative third. Give your top partners a named contact, a regular conversation and fresh, specific assets. This is time, not budget.
  • Economics last. Only after the above, compare your earnings per click with the programs your partners also promote. If you are genuinely behind, a targeted rate or bonus for proven partners is usually smarter than raising rates for everyone.

Then set up the early warning you were missing: a simple weekly check on your top partners' clicks, so the next slide gets a phone call in week one instead of a post-mortem in month six.

Where this fits in a full program review

Partner churn sits mostly in two elements of a program review: retention and tracking. But the causes often start in payout structure or creative, which is why it helps to rate the whole program rather than chase one symptom. Frequently asked questions

How do I know if an affiliate has stopped promoting my program?

Look at clicks, not just conversions. A partner whose clicks fall sharply while their site or channel is still active has usually moved your link to a less visible spot or replaced it. Compare each top partner's last 60 days with their own previous 60 days, not with the program average.

Should I just raise commissions to win affiliates back?

Not as a first move. If the real cause is tracking trust, a weak landing page or silence from your side, a higher rate will not bring the partner back and you will pay it to everyone. Diagnose the cause first, then decide whether economics is part of it.

Will affiliates tell me why they stopped?

Rarely on their own. Most partners simply reallocate traffic. If you ask directly and specifically, for example whether their conversion rate changed or whether they moved to a competitor, many will tell you. A vague check-in email usually gets no reply.

Is it normal for affiliate partners to churn?

Some churn is normal: sites get sold, creators change focus, niches shift. The problem is unexplained churn among your top partners, or churn nobody notices for months. That is a program issue, not a market one.

What does an affiliate program audit check for partner churn?

A good audit looks at payout competitiveness against the programs your partners also promote, tracking accuracy against your own sales data, landing page and offer conversion, payout reliability, partner communication and how top partners are segmented and managed. It ranks the causes by revenue impact so you know what to fix first.
