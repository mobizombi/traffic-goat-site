Affiliate Onboarding: What to Do in a New Partner's First 30 Days

Traffic Goat Insights - By Adam S.

Affiliate onboarding is the work between approving a partner and their first sale. In practice: approve fast and send a working link on day 0, deliver a short kit and test tracking in week one, set a first-sale target by partner type, check in personally on day 14, and review and segment every new partner on day 30.

Most programs treat approval as the finish line. The partner gets an automated email, a dashboard login and a link to a banner folder, and the next message they receive is the monthly newsletter. Whether they ever send a click is left to chance. We made the case that this is where most programs actually lose revenue in most affiliate programs do not have a traffic problem. This post is the practical follow-up: what to do, on which day, and who should do it.

Why the first 30 days decide most of it

A new partner joins with intent: a review they want to write, a list they want to mail, a comparison they are updating. That intent has a short shelf life. Every day without a working link, a clear starting point or a reply from a human makes it easier to move on to the next program in their queue. A partner who has not published in the first month has usually filed you under "later".

So the goal of onboarding is not a welcome. It is a first piece of promotion, a first click, and ideally a first sale, as early as the partner's channel allows.

Day 0: approval and the welcome

Day 0 is the day you approve the partner. Two things matter: how long they waited, and what the first message gives them.

  • Approve within a working day. Review applications daily, not weekly. If you need manual checks for fraud or fit, do them fast, and tell the applicant when they will hear back.
  • Send one working tracking link. Not a login and an instruction to generate one. Test it before it goes out.
  • Recommend one starting point. The page or offer that converts best for their partner type right now, with a sentence on why. A partner with one clear place to send traffic starts faster than a partner with a menu.
  • Name a person. A real name and a reply address that gets answered within a day. "noreply" at this stage tells the partner exactly how much attention to expect.
  • State the money in plain language. Commission, cookie window, payout threshold, payment dates and what causes a reversal. Partners compare these before they commit any effort.
  • Ask one question. "What are you planning to publish first, and when?" The answer tells you what they need, and the reply itself is the first sign of an active partner.

Days 1 to 7: the first-week kit and a tracking test

In the first week, the partner should have everything they need to publish their first piece without asking for anything. That means a kit sized to their channel, not the whole library.

The starter kit

  • Deep links to the three or four pages that matter for their channel: the recommended starting page, pricing, a free trial or demo page, and any current offer page.
  • A product facts sheet with current plans, prices, approved claims and the claims they must not make.
  • Two or three assets for their format. Swipe emails for an email partner, screenshots and a comparison table for a content site, talking points and a short code for a creator.
  • Current offers with dates, so they can plan their first piece around something live.

The tracking test

Before a partner sends real traffic, confirm their link works end to end. Click it from a clean browser session, check that the click registers in the platform under their account, follow every redirect to the landing page and checkout, and confirm the click ID is still there. For partners you expect to matter, place a test order and confirm the conversion is credited to them.

This takes a few minutes per partner and it prevents the worst possible start: a partner who publishes, sees clicks and no sales, and concludes your program does not convert. If anything fails, the fix list is in affiliate tracking issues: why partner sales go missing.

The first-sale target

"Get partners active" is not a target. Set a date by which you expect a first click and a first sale, per partner type, and write it down before the partner joins.

The right date depends on the channel and your sales cycle. An email partner can mail their list within days; a content site may need weeks to publish and get indexed; a product with a two-week trial sees first sales later than one people buy on the spot. Set each target from how the channel works, then adjust it from your own data.

The target does two jobs. It gives you a trigger: a partner who passes their first-click date with no clicks gets a nudge, not silence. And it gives you a number to report, the share of each month's new partners who hit their first sale by the target date, which tells you far more about program health than the count of approved accounts.

Day 14: the check-in

Day 14 is the moment most programs skip, and the one most worth keeping personal for partners who fit your profile. By now the partner has had the kit for a week. They have either started, are about to, or have stalled.

Look at their data first, then write one short, specific message:

  • If they have clicks: say what you see and offer the next step. "Your review is sending traffic to the pricing page. Readers from comparison content tend to do better on the plan comparison page; here is a link if you want to try it."
  • If they have published but have no clicks: check the placement and the link. A broken link, a link buried at the bottom of a page or a missing disclosure can all stop traffic before it starts.
  • If they have done nothing: refer back to their own plan. "You mentioned a comparison post this month. Is there anything you need from us to finish it: data, screenshots, a quote?" A specific offer of help gets a reply far more often than "just checking in".

Day 30: the review and segmenting

At day 30, every new partner gets a status and moves out of onboarding into the way they will be managed from then on. Pull clicks, conversions and revenue for each partner in that month's cohort, and sort them into four groups:

  • Producing. First sale made, or clear traffic that is converting. Move them to your active management cadence and ask what would help them do more: a custom landing page, an exclusive offer, early notice of promotions.
  • Started. Published and sending clicks, no sale yet. Look at the landing page and offer they are using. This group often needs a better destination, not more encouragement.
  • Stalled, good fit. No activity yet, but they match your target profile. One more personal message with a specific question about what is in the way. If there is no answer, move them to the light-touch group and try again next quarter.
  • Not a fit. No activity and no match with your profile. Move them to the newsletter list. Stop spending onboarding effort on them, and stop counting them as active partners.

Record two things for the cohort as a whole: the share that reached a first sale within 30 days, and where each of those partners came from. Over a few cohorts, that tells you which recruitment sources produce partners who start, and which onboarding step loses the most people. Partners who start well and later go quiet are a different problem; we covered those in why affiliates stop promoting your program.

What to automate and what to keep personal

Onboarding does not need a large team. It needs the repetitive parts automated so the personal parts get done.

Automate:

  • The welcome message, with the link and kit selected by partner type.
  • Kit delivery and a reminder a few days later for partners who have not opened it.
  • The first-click nudge for partners past their target date.
  • The data pull for the day 30 review, so the cohort table builds itself.

Keep personal:

  • The day 14 check-in for partners who fit your profile.
  • The day 30 conversation with partners who are producing or close to it.
  • Any reply to a partner question. An automated answer to a real question does more harm than a slow human one.

A useful test: if a message would read the same for every partner, automate it. If it only works because it mentions what this partner did, a person should write it.

A worked example: one month's cohort

This is an illustration with made-up round numbers, to show the reasoning. It is not a real program.

A subscription software company approves 40 partners in one month: 25 content sites, 10 newsletter owners and 5 creators. Its old onboarding was an automated welcome and a link to the banner folder. It switches to the plan above.

  • Day 0: approvals go out within a day, each with a tested link to the plan comparison page and a named contact. Twelve partners reply to the "what will you publish first" question.
  • Week one: the tracking test catches a redirect on the free trial page that drops the click ID. It is fixed before any partner links to it.
  • Day 14: eight partners have clicks. Of the stalled ones, the team writes personally to the fifteen who fit its target profile. Several ask for the same thing: a comparison table against the two main alternatives. The team builds it and adds it to the kit.
  • Day 30: the cohort sorts into producing, started, stalled good fit and not a fit.

The useful output is not the count. It is three decisions for next month: keep the comparison page as the default starting point, add the comparison table to every content-site kit on day 1 instead of on request, and set a later first-sale target for content sites so they are not nudged before they could reasonably have published. Common affiliate onboarding mistakes

  • Treating approval as onboarding. An approval email and a login is the start of onboarding, not the whole of it.
  • Slow approvals. A partner who waits a week for a decision has often already started with someone else.
  • Sending everything at once. The full library on day 0 buries the one link and the one starting point that matter.
  • No tracking test. The first sale that goes uncredited is often the last effort a partner makes.
  • No end point. Without a day 30 review, inactive partners stay in the "new" group forever and inflate your active partner count.
  • Measuring sign-ups instead of first sales. A growing approved list with a flat first-sale rate means onboarding is the bottleneck, not recruitment.

Frequently asked questions

What is affiliate onboarding?

Affiliate onboarding is everything a program does between approving a partner and that partner's first sale: the welcome message, a working tracking link, the starter kit of links and creative, a tracking test, a personal check-in and a review at the end of the first month. Its job is to turn an approved account into an active partner.

How long should affiliate onboarding take?

Plan it as 30 days. The first week covers the welcome, the starter kit and a tracking test, the second and third weeks aim at a first click and a first sale, and day 30 is a review where each new partner is sorted into a segment that decides how they are managed from then on.

What should an affiliate welcome email include?

A working tracking link, one recommended starting page or offer, the two or three assets that suit the partner's channel, a link to the full resource kit, the payout terms in plain language, a named contact who replies, and one clear first step with a date. Leave out anything the partner does not need in the first week.

How soon should a new affiliate make a first sale?

It depends on the channel and your sales cycle, so set the target per partner type rather than one number for everyone. An email partner can often send traffic within days, while a content site may need weeks to publish and rank. What matters is that each partner type has a target date and that someone looks at who missed it.

Should affiliate onboarding be automated?

Automate the parts that are the same for everyone: the welcome message, kit delivery, reminders for partners who have not clicked their link, and the data pull for the day 30 review. Keep the check-in and the day 30 conversation personal for partners who fit your target profile, because those are the moments that decide whether they start.

What should you do with affiliates who are still inactive after 30 days?

Sort them. Partners who fit your profile but have not started get one specific, personal message asking what is in the way. Partners who do not fit move to a light-touch newsletter list. Do not keep spending onboarding effort on accounts that show no sign of starting, and do not count them as active partners in your reporting.
